Afraid a loss mitigation application won't count

Bracing for them to say your application doesn't count.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · August 23, 2026

The kitchen table stopped being a table a while ago. It is piles now: bank statements, pay stubs, a hardship letter you have rewritten three times, and somewhere in the middle of it all, the word that brought you here. Complete. Because you are not really asking what to send. You are asking what makes it impossible for them to say you didn't send it. That edge in your question is earned. Maybe you already submitted a packet once and got told it was incomplete, with no explanation. Maybe you have just heard enough stories about servicers losing paperwork while the foreclosure clock keeps running.

Either way, here is what I am going to hand you today. Complete is not their mood. It is a legal line, defined in federal rule and again in Colorado law, with deadlines and duties hanging off it, and nearly all of those duties land on them, not you. By the end of this page you will know where the line is, how to force their list into writing, and what happens to your file if the company holding it changes in the middle of everything.

The list is theirs, and that is exactly what scared you

Start with the definition, because everything else hangs on it. Your loss mitigation application is complete when your servicer has received all the information it requires from you to evaluate you for the options available on your loan (12 CFR 1024.41(b)(1)). Colorado law defines it the same way. Notice what that means: there is no universal, printable government checklist. The list is your servicer's list, and it varies, because the owner of your loan, not the servicer, is who decides which kinds of help are even on the table for you. Two people with the same hardship can be asked for different stacks of paper.

I can still tell you the shape most packets share. Nearly all of them ask you to describe the hardship, the thing that changed: a job loss, a disability, medical bills insurance didn't cover, a divorce, a death in the family. And they generally want you to be able to back that story up with evidence, a divorce decree, the checks that paid the hospital, whatever shows it is real.

Around that sits the financial picture they request from you, which is what those piles on your table already are. But the honest answer to what do I need to send is not a list I can print here. It is their list, matched exactly. I know that sounds like the trap you came in bracing for. Keep reading, because it is not allowed to be one.

They have to tell you what is missing, in writing

Here is the mechanism that turns their list from a moving target into a document. Get your application in 45 days or more before any foreclosure sale, and your servicer must promptly review it and tell you in writing, within five business days, whether it is complete. If it is not, that notice has to name the specific documents and information still missing, and it has to give you a reasonable date to get them in (12 CFR 1024.41(b)(2)). Not a hint. Not a phone call you can't prove happened. A list, on paper, with your name on it.

And the guessing is not supposed to be yours to carry. The same rule requires your servicer to use reasonable diligence to gather what it needs to complete your application. Colorado repeats that duty in its own law and attaches a person to it: by day 45 of falling behind, your servicer must give you a single point of contact, one person whose actual job includes telling you what a complete application requires, where yours stands, and which deadlines apply (CRS 38-38-103.1). You are allowed to make that person do their job.

One more thing, because you have either been burned or heard from people who were. Homeowners who have learned this the hard way almost always do two things: they send everything in a way that leaves a record, and they keep a copy of every page and every date. Not because a rule demands it. Because a paper trail turns we never received that from a door they can close on you into a claim they have to defend.

The trapdoor you are bracing for has a lock

Now the fear with teeth in it. You match their written list, and a month later someone new finds one more thing, and the whole file quietly resets to zero. The rule saw that move coming. The moment you submit everything the servicer's notice asked for, your application becomes what the law calls facially complete.

If the servicer later discovers it genuinely needs more information or a corrected document, it has to ask you promptly. And while you are given a reasonable chance to respond, it must keep treating your application as complete, with every protection that status carries. Send in the missing piece within that window, and your application counts as complete all the way back to the day it first became facially complete (12 CFR 1024.41(c)(2)(iv)). In plain words, matching their list locks a door behind you. A late discovery can add a step. It cannot erase your place in line.

One nearby protection deserves a breath. Your servicer generally cannot decide your whole case off an incomplete application; the real evaluation is supposed to happen on a complete one. The narrow exception is a short-term fix, a brief forbearance or repayment plan, which can be offered on an incomplete file. If that happens, the offer letter has to say it was based on an incomplete application. Take the breathing room if it helps you. Just know the letter itself has to tell you that other options may exist, and that finishing your file still gets you the full review.

Leverage you can finally hold in your hand

So what does crossing the line actually get you? A complete application received more than 37 days before a foreclosure sale forces a real answer: within 30 days, your servicer must evaluate you for every option available on your loan and tell you in writing which ones, if any, it will offer (12 CFR 1024.41(c)(1)). While that on-time, complete application is pending, they generally cannot push the foreclosure through to a sale. And if you got yours in 90 days or more ahead, a denied loan modification comes with a right to appeal, reviewed by different people than the ones who said no.

One honest line so nothing here overpromises. Complete earns you a full evaluation, not an approval. No law forces your servicer to grant any particular option (12 CFR 1024.41(a)). What the law forces is a real review, on a deadline, in writing. That is smaller than a promise, and much bigger than nothing.

And Colorado built you a receipt. If you are holding your servicer's written confirmation of a complete application, dated on the right side of that same 37-day line, and the foreclosure keeps moving anyway, you can present that confirmation to the public trustee as late as 14 calendar days before the sale. The officer must then take the question to your servicer and postpone the sale until the servicer answers (CRS 38-38-103.2(3)). So hold on to that letter when it comes. It is not a courtesy note. It is paper strong enough to hold up a sale.

The fear your file dies in the handoff

If the company you have been dealing with just changed, I know which version of this fear you are carrying: that everything you fought to assemble is sitting in a box nobody at the new company will ever open. The law says otherwise. When servicing transfers, your application transfers with its clock still running. The new servicer steps into the old one's deadlines, counted from the day the old servicer received your application, and every right attached to it, the evaluation, the foreclosure protections, the appeal, crosses over intact (12 CFR 1024.41(k)).

The specifics have teeth. If you were still owed the complete-or-incomplete letter when the switch happened, the new servicer has ten business days from the transfer date to send it, and it cannot make the first foreclosure filing until the completion date it gave you has passed. If your application was already complete, the new servicer gets that same 30-day evaluation window, counted from the transfer date this time, to finish the review and hand you the written decision.

And that single point of contact is required to retrieve, in a timely manner, the written information you already provided, including what you sent to the previous servicer when it is available. You do not start over. The file follows the loan, and so do your rights.

Two quick distinctions so the mail makes sense. A servicing transfer means the company you deal with changed, and it comes with notice, generally a letter at least 15 days before the switch. A sale of the loan itself means the debt has a new owner, and that owner must tell you in writing, within 30 days of the sale, who they are and how to reach someone with real authority over the loan (15 U.S.C. 1641(g)). Either way, nobody's paperwork shuffle erases what you already sent.

You are making it count

There is nothing glamorous about what you are doing at that table, and maybe that is why nobody has told you it is brave. Paperwork, under pressure, for the same company that is foreclosing on you. That takes a steadiness most people never have to find. And while you wait on their answer, you do not have to wait on your whole future, because we at Transitus can show you every option you have for your home. Whatever they decide, you will already know where you stand. You have done the hard part. Make it count.
