Curious if my inherited home owes capital gains

Wondering if the house they left comes with a tax bill.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · October 21, 2026

There are two numbers you keep putting next to each other during this hard time. What your parents paid for the house, a lifetime ago, and what it would sell for now as they left. The first part doesn’t feel nearly as important as the second but you’re pushing yourself to be here. And I see that. The math you keep running says the IRS will treat this home as your profit the day you sell. And this house is the last thing they handed you, and it feels like grief might come with a bill. So you stopped to check before signing anything, and I am glad you did, because the checking ends well.

Nothing has been owed this whole time

Set the first fear down, because it covers everything that has already happened. Inheriting the house was not a taxable event. Federal law says gross income does not include the value of property received by inheritance (26 U.S.C. 102). Nobody gets an income tax bill for being left a home. The federal estate tax lives in a different universe than most families: a return is only required when the estate is worth more than $13,990,000 for a death in 2025, or $15,000,000 for 2026. And Colorado adds nothing of its own. There is no Colorado estate tax for current deaths and no inheritance tax at all. So the house has not been quietly building a debt against you since the funeral. The one moment the IRS ever looks at is the sale, and even then, only at the gain. Which turns everything on one question: measured from what?

The rumor you came to verify is true

Measured from the day they died. The day that still sits with you today and will forever. That is the stepped-up basis, and it is not a loophole or a maybe. Under federal law, property you inherit takes a cost basis equal to its fair market value on the date of death, not what the original owner paid for it (26 U.S.C. 1014(a)). All the growth that happened on their watch does not impact you whatsoever. As far as the IRS is concerned, you received the house at its present-day value.

One footnote so the term cannot confuse you later. If the estate's executor elected something called alternate valuation on the estate's own return, the measuring date moves to roughly six months after the death instead of the date of death. Very few estates ever make that election but I wanted you to know of its existence in case.

Smaller than the math you kept running

So the taxable part is the growth between the death and your closing, if the market gave you any. Even that slice gets the gentler treatment. Gain on inherited property automatically counts as long-term no matter how fast you sell: the tax code treats an heir who sells within a year of the death as having held the property for more than a year (26 U.S.C. 1223(9)). There is no penalty for selling quickly, and no reason to sit on the house just to reach some holding period. Long-term matters because the rates are the mild ones. For 2025, the federal long-term capital gains rate is 0% on taxable income up to $48,350 for a single filer, or $96,700 filing jointly. It is 15% from there up to $533,400 single, $600,050 joint, and 20% only above that. Most sellers pay 15% or less, and only on the post-death slice.

Colorado keeps its share simple. The gain rides into your state income and is taxed at Colorado's flat income tax rate, 4.4% for tax year 2025. No separate state capital gains tax. Add it up honestly: for an heir selling in the same season the estate settles, the bill is usually a fraction of the kitchen-table math, and often it is zero.

One page of the trust can change this

Before anyone counts on the reset, there is one piece of paperwork I want you to see: how the house traveled to you. Through a will, through the probate estate, or through an ordinary revocable living trust, the kind parents use to skip probate, the step-up applies. The law expressly covers property the owner put in a trust they kept the power to revoke or amend (26 U.S.C. 1014(b)(2)-(3)).

The exception is a trust that was already irrevocable during your parent's life. For those, the IRS has ruled there is no step-up when the trust's assets sit outside the taxable estate: the basis the day after death matches the basis the day before (Rev. Rul. 2023-2). If your documents say irrevocable from the start, that is the one corner here where families put the papers in front of a tax professional before the house is listed. For everyone else, the reset is yours.

Hoping there is a way around the rest

If a real gain is still sitting there, maybe prices climbed since the death, or the sale is still a year or two away, two legitimate doors exist, and each has a condition. The first is the home-sale exclusion, the shield of up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 on a qualifying joint return. It belongs only to someone who owned the home and lived in it as their main residence for at least two of the five years before the sale (26 U.S.C. 121). An heir who never moves in does not qualify. An heir who moves in and stays long enough can.

The second is a 1031 exchange, which can defer gain only on property held for business or investment, an inherited house turned rental, for example, traded for other investment real estate (26 U.S.C. 1031(a)(1)). It is not available for a personal-use home. Neither door is required, and neither is a trick. For most heirs, the step-up already did the heavy lifting, and these exist for the gain that grew after it.

What they left you can reach you whole

You paused in the middle of grief to make sure a mistake would not eat what they left, and that carefulness honors them. You can let the concern rest now. I hope what reaches you feels like what they meant you to have and that you can work through this moment with family and friends.
