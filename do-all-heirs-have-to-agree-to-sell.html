Stuck on whether all heirs have to agree to sell

Concerned if one heir says no the house just sits.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · November 23, 2026

The paperwork is ready. The listing agreement, or the contract itself. And one line on it stays empty, because one person in the family will not sign, and you have been careful not to say their name out loud, because naming them turns a disagreement into a feud. So you went looking for a rule instead. Not advice. Not another way to win the argument. A rule. Something outside the family that settles this, because persuasion has run its course and the house sits there costing someone money every month it waits. Maybe you are the one the court put in charge. Or maybe you are the one whose no everyone keeps asking about. Both of you deserve the real answer, and I will give it to you straight.

No, and I know that answer cuts two ways

In Colorado probate, the answer is no. The heirs do not all need to agree before the house can be sold. Once the court appoints a personal representative, the person most families call the executor, that representative holds the same power over the title to estate property that an absolute owner would have, and the law lets them use it without notice, a hearing, or a court order (CRS 15-12-711). Selling the home, at a public or private sale, sits expressly inside those powers unless the will itself or a court order restricts them (CRS 15-1-804(2)(g)(I)). Nowhere does the law make the family's agreement a condition.

And the signature being withheld at your kitchen table is not the one a closing actually needs. The sale closes on the representative's signature. I know it’s not that simple for you and your family and I see that but the law makes it that simple.

But the house already belongs to us

If you are wondering how this can possibly be legal, when the will or the bloodline already made the house partly yours, you are not wrong about the ownership. When someone dies in Colorado, their property does pass immediately to the people named in the will, or to their heirs if there is no will. But it passes subject to administration: the rights of creditors, the protections for a surviving spouse and family, the settling of the estate itself (CRS 15-12-101). During probate, the house is under administration. You own something real. You do not hold the house free and clear yet.

That is the whole mechanism. The title power was never handed to the family as a vote. It was placed in one person's hands, held in trust for everyone with an interest in the estate, so that an estate with five opinions can still get settled. One no does not freeze it, and a stack of yeses does not speed it up.

Hoping, or dreading, you are the exception

Before anyone acts on that answer, check it against the situations where a no really does rule, because they change everything.

The first is a house the will leaves to a specific person. The general power to sell the estate's real property does not reach property that is specifically devised (CRS 15-1-804(2)(g)). If the will names your sister and hands her the house itself, the executor's ordinary sale power stops at that door. A house that simply falls into the leftover share of the estate, split among the children, gets no such carve-out.

The second is co-executors. When two or more personal representatives are appointed together, the law requires all of them to concur on the acts of administering and distributing the estate unless the will says otherwise (CRS 15-12-717). The outs are narrow: true emergencies when everyone cannot be reached in time, receiving property owed to the estate, or a co-representative formally delegated to act for the others. So if you and your brother were appointed together, his no is not just a family position. It is a legal one, and the house does not sell without him.

And one quieter boundary. All of this power begins at appointment, not at the funeral. Someone who is only named in a will, and has not yet been appointed by the court or registrar, has no authority to sell anything (CRS 15-12-701).

Afraid your no counts for nothing

If you are the one refusing to sign, I have not forgotten you, and I won't pretend the sections above read gently from your chair. Your agreement is not required. But your no is not weightless, because the power you are up against comes on a leash.

A personal representative is a fiduciary. The law holds them to the standards of care that apply to trustees and requires them to use their authority for the best interests of everyone who succeeds to the estate, settling it as expeditiously and efficiently as those interests allow (CRS 15-12-703(1)). Hear both halves of that. The same statute that pushes them to get the estate settled, instead of letting it bleed, also means the power to sell over your objection is not a license to sell badly. Not for a careless price, not on a whim, not as a favor to a buyer they like.

And that same leash is why representatives standing in this exact stalemate commonly keep the fairness on paper: the appraisal, the listing history, the offers, gathered where any heir can look. Not because consent is required. Because the family is going to outlast the closing, and trust is worth building wherever it still can be. The family on the other side of the closing

You came here asking the law to break a tie, and it does. But I know what the statute cannot touch: you are not just trying to settle a house, you are trying to come out the other side still a family, and that was never going to be decided by a signature. I hope this hard moment works out as well as it possibly can for you and your family.
