Afraid to misstep selling your inherited house

Ready to sell, hoping to do it right.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · October 25, 2026

Somewhere in the last few weeks the house stopped being a place and became a task. There is a list now. Utilities to move, rooms to empty, tax bills still arriving in a name that hurts to read. And at the top of the list, the thought of selling. You are not asking if. You are asking how, because you can feel there is something different about this sale and you would rather learn it today than meet it mid-contract. I see that care, and it is exactly right. An inherited house sells the way any house sells but the death changes who is allowed to sign.

The thing you can feel but cannot name yet

Here is the ground floor, because it explains everything after it. In Colorado, when a person dies, what they owned passes immediately to the people their will names, or to their heirs when there is no will. But on paper only. The estate’s debts get paid before anyone truly keeps anything, and nobody can sign for the house until the court names someone (CRS 15-12-101). If Medicaid paid for their nursing home care, that bill is one of them, and it has walls.

That gap, between the house being yours and the title being ready to sign, is the only real difference between this sale and a normal one. And the size of the gap depends on which of the four roads carried the house to you: a co-owner's name already on the deed, a beneficiary deed waiting in the county records, a living trust, or probate. The first three close the gap almost on their own. The fourth is a process. The paperwork decides which road is yours, so that is where this starts.

The relief of finding it was already yours

Start with the deed itself. If your name was on it with theirs as joint tenants, the way most married couples and some parents and children hold a home, the house finished traveling the moment they died. A deceased joint tenant's interest simply ends, and the survivor’s continues by law (CRS 38-31-101(6)(c)). No probate. No one's permission. The house is yours the way it was yours last month, just more alone now, and I am sorry for how that version of ownership arrives.

One piece of housekeeping makes the record match: the death certificate gets recorded with the county clerk, along with a short affidavit identifying the property and the person who died (CRS 38-31-102(1)). Once that is on file, the title is clear, and you sell like any other owner.

Checking whether they planned this for you

Two other roads skip the court entirely, and both exist because they planned ahead. The first is a beneficiary deed: a deed the owner recorded before death naming who receives the property, with the transfer taking effect only when they die (CRS 15-15-402). If one exists, it is sitting in the county records right now. One honest edge: the house arrives carrying whatever mortgage and liens were already on it (CRS 15-15-407). The deed skips probate, not the debts.

The second is a living trust. If the house sits in one, the road is the shortest of all: the trust's trustee is the seller, and no court has to approve the sale (CRS 15-5-815(1)). If the county records show neither, and your name was not on the deed, the house takes the common road: probate.

So it does have to go through the court

If that word dropped something in your chest or you have never heard it, I understand. But, it is a road families finish every day. So breathe. In Colorado, a will cannot transfer the house, and cannot even officially make anyone the personal representative, until probate opens and the will is declared valid (CRS 15-12-102). Opening probate puts one person in charge of the estate, the personal representative. Colorado fills that seat in a set order of priority, starting with the person the will names, then moving through the surviving spouse and other family (CRS 15-12-203(1)). For most families it never gets past the first name.

And here is the part I most want you to carry. Once appointed, the personal representative holds the same power over estate property that an outright owner would have (CRS 15-12-711). That power expressly includes selling the estate's real property, at public or private sale, on fair and reasonable terms (CRS 15-1-804(2)(g)(I)). The personal representative signs the listing, the contract, and the closing. Not the heirs voting. Not a judge.

Two edges so nothing ambushes you later. If the will leaves the house to one named person specifically, the general sale power does not cover it; the house belongs to that person. And the small-estate affidavit you may have heard about, the shortcut that lets families collect bank accounts and belongings without probate, covers personal property only. It cannot move real estate (CRS 15-12-1201(1)). For a house, there is no affidavit around the process.

Choosing whether the estate sells it or you do

From inside probate, the sale can end two ways, and both are ordinary. The first: the estate sells the house while it is open. The personal representative lists it, takes the offer, and signs the closing, and the money comes back to the estate, where it pays what the estate owes before the rest divides among the heirs. For a family that already knows the house is leaving, this is the straightest line, because nothing waits on the estate finishing first.

The second: the house comes out of the estate before it sells. The personal representative executes a deed of distribution, the recorded document that moves title into an heir's own name (CRS 15-12-907). From that day, the heir sells whenever they choose, like any owner. If several of you received it together and cannot agree on selling, that deadlock has its own answer. Both work, and working with a probate lawyer will help you determine what is best for you and your family.

The bills that did not stop for any of this

One more honest piece, because it is probably the pressure you feel most. The mortgage did not end with them. The lender keeps its lien on the house and can still foreclose if the loan goes unpaid, probate or no probate (CRS 15-12-803(3)(a)). I see that burden, but moving swiftly through this process can protect you. And if you are worried about a future tax bill from selling, the math resets the day they died and protects you there also. The task can go back to being a task

You took on the one job they could not do for you, and you came here to learn it before signing a thing. That care honors them, and it is most of the job already done. I hope the sale carries lightly, and I hope what they left you reaches you whole.
