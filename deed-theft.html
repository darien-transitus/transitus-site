Stunned by deed theft on the house you own

The paper that says your house isn't yours.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · August 30, 2026

You may be standing in your own kitchen, reading a letter that says the house around you belongs to someone else. Or you pulled the county record and found a stranger's name on a deed, above a signature you never wrote. Everything in you is saying this should not be possible. Keep that instinct. It is correct. Anyone can file a piece of paper, and yes, someone can claim your house. What a claim cannot do is turn a lie into ownership. I promise you that.

It is real, and you were targeted, not careless

What happened to you has a name: deed theft. The FTC describes it as identity theft, because that is what it is. Someone pretends to be you, fakes your signature on a deed, and records it, hoping the paper looks official enough that nobody questions it. And the people who run this scheme choose their targets. Homeowners already in foreclosure get hunted for it, because a foreclosure filing is public record, and it hands a thief a list of people who are stressed, stretched thin, and fighting on another front. If that is you, please hear this: you were picked and none of this is your fault.

In Colorado, forging a deed is a felony (CRS 18-5-102). Not a gray area, not a clever trick that holds if you fail to catch it in time. The person claiming your house is not a rival owner with a competing story. They are committing a crime, and the law treats them accordingly.

The paper is weaker than it looks

I want to take apart the fear that a stamp made the lie official. Recording a deed does not make it true. The clerk who filed it was not ruling on your signature; recording offices file what they are handed, and the stamp only says a document arrived, not that it is honest. Colorado even has a legal name for a fake like this. It’s called a spurious document and it’s one that is forged, groundless, or patently invalid.

And there is a clock already running in your favor. A spurious document cannot hold or affect your property for more than 35 days after it is recorded unless, inside that window, the person behind it files a court action to enforce it and records notice of that lawsuit (CRS 38-35-203(1)). Read that again slowly. To keep the claim alive, a thief has to carry their forgery into a courtroom and argue for it in front of a judge. That is the last room a forger wants to stand in.

For once, the process moves fast for you

You do not have to wait them out, either. Colorado built an expedited path for exactly this. As the owner, you can petition the district court in the county where the fake deed was recorded for a hearing. This hearing must be set 14 to 21 days after the papers are served (CRS 38-35-204). Weeks. Not the years of litigation you may be bracing for.

If the court finds the document is spurious, the judge must declare it invalid, order it released from the record, and enter a money judgment for your costs, including reasonable attorney fees, against the person who recorded it. That last piece matters, and it is part of why people in your position rarely walk this petition in alone. The law expects the thief to pay for the lawyer who beats them.

Colorado puts a price on the act itself too. Anyone who records a document they know or should know is forged owes the owner at least $1,000 or the actual damages, whichever is greater, plus attorney fees, and the same liability lands on anyone who willfully refuses to release the fake deed once you ask them to (CRS 38-35-109(3)). This terrible situation is at least covered well by the law.

The fear the ads sold you

Maybe nothing has happened to your house at all. Maybe an ad or a news story sent you here, asking whether your title can be stolen silently while you sleep, and whether you need to pay a company to guard it. Here is what the FTC itself says about those title lock products: they are not insurance and they are not a lock. They cannot prevent a transfer of your title. They are monitoring services that alert you after a document has already been recorded, and they charge you for it.

The quieter truth sits underneath the marketing. You can check your title for free through your county's land records office, whenever you want. Denver's Clerk and Recorder runs FraudSleuth, a free alert service that emails you when a document matching your name gets recorded. Jefferson County runs a free Recording Notification Service that does the same. And Jefferson County says out loud what the ads never will: title fraud does happen but very infrequently. Real enough to watch for. Far smaller than the fear being sold to you.

The people whose job this already is

If a forged deed is sitting on your record right now, nobody expects you to untangle it alone, because this is bigger than a paperwork errand. It is a crime, so it belongs with the police; Denver, for example, directs suspected recording fraud straight to the Denver Police Department.

Homeowners who feel defrauded in a real estate matter also call the Mortgage Fraud Information Center at the Colorado Attorney General's office at (800) 222-4500. Colorado Legal Services exists for legal help when hiring an attorney feels out of reach. And because this is identity theft, the FTC's IdentityTheft.gov builds victims a free, personalized recovery plan. Every one of those doors is free to knock on you just need to knock.

It is still your house

Someone tried to take the one thing you were sure of with a pen, and you have every right to be shaken by that. But shaken is not the same as wrong. The truth of who owns your home did not move when that paper was filed, and the law knows it. Hold your ground. This is your home, no one else's.
