Unsure if selling a house in probate is allowed

Wondering if the house is even yours to sell yet.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · October 17, 2026

Nobody applies for this job. It arrived with a death of someone important to you, and it came with homework: a house full of their life, bills that keep printing their name, maybe a brother or sister waiting to hear a plan. The grieving is hard enough. And over all of it hangs one court word, probate. You have been careful. You have signed nothing. Part of you is afraid that touching the house too early is the mistake that blows up the estate or hands the family a fight. So take the answer with you before anything else. In Colorado, yes. A house can be sold during probate.

A house that is yours and not yours yet

Your hesitation is not timidity. It is an accurate reading of Colorado law. When someone dies here, what they owned passes immediately to the people named in the will, or to their heirs if there was no will. But it passes with strings attached: the transfer is subject to administration, to creditors, to family allowances. Nobody takes the house free and clear at the moment of death (CRS 15-12-101).

And the will itself, the paper everyone treats as the final word, proves nothing on its own. Until probate is opened and the will is declared valid, it cannot transfer the house and cannot even officially make anyone the executor (CRS 15-12-102). So the in-between feeling you have been living is legally real. The house is coming to someone. It has not finished arriving. Which means opening probate is not the thing that freezes the house. It is the thing that unfreezes it.

Waiting on the court to say it's you

The authority to sell belongs to one person: the personal representative, the role other states call the executor. And that authority does not begin at the funeral, or at the reading of the will, or when the family agrees you are the obvious one. It begins at appointment. Until the court or the registrar appoints you, a person nominated in a will has no power to sell estate property (CRS 15-12-701). That is the line you have been sensing, and now you can see exactly where it sits.

Two softer truths around that line. First, appointment is usually closer than the word court suggests. Colorado allows informal appointment through the registrar, paperwork rather than a hearing, and it fully establishes a personal representative's powers (CRS 15-12-307). The law also sets the order of who may serve, starting with the person the will names, then the surviving spouse and other close family (CRS 15-12-203(1)). Second, if you have already been paying the utilities and keeping the place safe, breathe. The law lets a personal representative ratify earlier acts done for the estate's benefit, so looking after the house before your appointment is not the misstep you may have feared.

The relief that during really means during

Here is what appointment actually hands you. Once appointed, a Colorado personal representative has the same power over estate property that an absolute owner would have, and in an ordinary, unsupervised estate that power can be exercised without notice, without a hearing, and without a court order (CRS 15-12-711). You do not petition a judge for permission to list the house. You do not wait for probate to close.

The sale power is spelled out too: the estate's real property can be sold and conveyed, at public or private sale, on fair and reasonable terms (CRS 15-1-804(2)(g)(I)). And the law actually pushes in the direction you were hoping, directing a personal representative to proceed expeditiously with settling the estate (CRS 15-12-704). Two honest edges, so nothing ambushes you at a closing table. If the will leaves the house specifically to a named person, that general sale power does not cover it (CRS 15-12-715(1)(a)); a specifically devised house is on its own track. And if the court has placed the estate under supervised administration, your powers continue unless the court restricts them, and a restriction has to be endorsed on your letters of appointment to count against outsiders (CRS 15-12-504). If neither edge is yours, the ordinary case is the simple one: appointed, empowered, allowed to sell.

The fear the sale falls apart later

Maybe the deeper worry is not whether you can sign, but whether a signature made mid-probate holds. Whether a buyer walks away, or a title company flags it, or the whole thing gets unwound a year from now by someone unhappy with how it went. Colorado answered that one directly. A buyer who deals with a personal representative in good faith and for value is protected as if the power was exercised properly, and the buyer is not required to dig into the will or question your authority (CRS 15-12-714(1)). That protection is the reason title companies can insure probate sales. A sale during probate is not a gray-market transaction that careful people avoid. It is an ordinary closing with one extra document in the file: your letters.

Selling with the whole family watching

Now the part that makes your pen heavier than a normal seller's. I see it. The power you hold is held in trust. You are a fiduciary, held to the standards of a trustee, and the estate's best interests are the measure of every move, the sale of the house included (CRS 15-12-703(1)). In plain terms: the sale money is the estate's, not yours. It pays what the estate owes first, and what remains gets distributed to the people the will or the law names. That can be a heavy burden to carry, but you were chosen for a reason.

And the law draws one bright line exactly where family fights start. A sale of estate property to yourself, your spouse, your agent, or anything you hold an interest in is voidable by any interested person who did not consent, unless the will authorized it or the court approves it after notice (CRS 15-12-713(1)). Read that as protection, not suspicion. An open sale at a real price, documented, is what the statute rewards. What it punishes is the quiet sweetheart deal. If a sibling is watching your every move, this is the rule that lets your every move hold up.

The hope of selling before probate even starts

Some of you are asking the more impatient but understandable version: can the house be sold before probate, or without it entirely? For a house, generally no, and I would rather you hear that plainly than lose a month to it. Colorado's small-estate shortcut, the affidavit families use to collect bank accounts and belongings, works only for personal property. It cannot transfer real estate (CRS 15-12-1201(1)). If the house has to pass through the estate, probate is the road.

There is one true exception worth knowing about. If the owner recorded a beneficiary deed before death, naming who gets the house, the property passes outside probate entirely (CRS 15-15-402). People in your spot usually check the county records early to see whether that deed exists, because it changes which rules you are living under. One honest note if it does: the house arrives with its mortgage and liens still attached (CRS 15-15-407).

The bills that don't pause for grief

I want to be straight about one more thing. The mortgage on the house did not die with its owner. The lien survives, and the lender can still foreclose if the loan goes unpaid, probate or no probate (CRS 15-12-803(3)(a)). The taxes, the insurance, the utilities keep accruing in the meantime, and often it is you fronting them.

One shield while you work. For a family home, federal law stops the lender from calling the whole loan due just because the house passed to a relative at death (12 U.S.C. 1701j-3(d)). The payments still have to be made, but the loan itself can stay in place while the estate moves. That is the honest shape of your timeline: the house's costs do not wait for the court, and the law does not make you wait either.

Allowed, and sooner than you feared

You were handed a job and a grief at the same time, and you have been careful with both. Careful enough to ask before touching anything. That is not weakness. That is you doing right by someone who trusted you with their affairs. I hope the settling, when it is done, leaves you with more peace than paperwork.
