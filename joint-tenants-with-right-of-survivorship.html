Grieving, hoping right of survivorship is real

Afraid the house is only half yours now.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · October 25, 2026

There are two names on the deed, and you are grieving one of them. The house itself has not changed. Same rooms, same key, same payment going out. What changed is the ground underneath it: a home you thought you simply shared, and now you are not certain you simply own. Nobody warns you that grief arrives with paperwork attached, or how heavy it is to not know whether a court, a will, or someone else's heirs now have a say in where you sleep. So before another night of wondering, let me show you what the law actually did the day they died. Almost all of it is gentler than what you have been bracing for.

The relief that it is already yours

If your deed names the two of you as joint tenants, here is what happened to the house the moment they died: it became fully yours. Not eventually. Not after a process. By law, a deceased joint tenant's interest simply ends, and the surviving joint tenant's interest continues free of it (CRS 38-31-101(6)(c)). There is no court step the house has to pass through, no probate it is sitting inside, no half of it waiting for someone else to claim. The ownership moved on its own, at the moment of death, the way the deed always promised it would.

Everything rests on words chosen years ago

Now the part I need you to check rather than assume, because Colorado is strict about it. A deed to two or more people does not create a joint tenancy unless it says so. The deed has to declare the property is conveyed in joint tenancy, or to you as joint tenants; without those words, Colorado's default is a different arrangement called tenancy in common (CRS 38-31-101). And a tenant in common's share does not go to the other owner on the deed. When a tenant in common dies, their share passes to whoever their will names, or to their heirs if there is no will, through the probate estate (CRS 15-12-101).

I know this is the catch you came in dreading, so let me size it honestly. For married couples who bought together, the joint tenancy wording is the common way homes get titled, so the odds are on your side. But the deed is the only thing that settles it, and it settles it completely. Pull your copy, or get one from the clerk and recorder of the county where the home sits, and read the line after your names.

If it says joint tenants, everything above is yours. If it does not, probate is the road their share travels. That is not the answer you wanted, and I won't pretend it is. But it is not the house slipping away either. The deed just decides whether it arrives on its own or through a process.

A smaller step than the dread around it

Here is a small but important step. Yes, the house is already yours but this step makes the county's records say so. You record two things with the clerk and recorder of the county where the property sits: the death certificate, and a short supplementary affidavit (CRS 38-31-102(1)).

The affidavit is sworn by a person of legal age with personal knowledge of the facts, and it carries two pieces: the legal description of the property, and a statement that the person named in the death certificate is the same person named on the recorded deed that created the joint tenancy. That is the whole document. One page tying the certificate to the deed.

And if it has been weeks or months and you have not filed anything, breathe. Not filing has not cost you the house; the recording only teaches the public record what already happened. People usually get it on file before a sale or a refinance, because that is when a clean record matters most. And if a sale is somewhere in your future, the tax bill on an inherited share is kinder than most people fear. Until then, nothing about the waiting has changed what you own.

The mortgage keeps going like nothing happened

One thing I want you to hear clearly. If there is still a mortgage, the loan did not end with them. The lien stays on the house, the monthly payment keeps coming due, and keeping it current is what keeps all of this quiet. And if some part of you worries the bank can now demand the whole balance or force you to refinance, it can't. Federal law bars a lender from calling the loan over a joint tenant's death (12 U.S.C. 1701j-3(d)). You keep paying it the way the two of you always did.

If the name beside yours is an ex-spouse

One more turn of this rule, because old deeds outlive marriages. In Colorado, a divorce automatically severs the former spouses' joint tenancy, turning it into a tenancy in common, unless a court order, a marital agreement, or the document itself says otherwise (CRS 15-11-804(2)(b)). So if the co-owner who died is someone you divorced, the survivorship promise on that old deed stopped working when the divorce was final. Their half does not flow to you; it goes through their estate. And it runs the other way too: an ex whose name never came off your deed does not take your home by survivorship either.

The house can go back to being home

You have been grieving a person and auditing a deed in the same week, and nobody should have to do both at once. Whatever the paperwork still asks of you, it is smaller than what you are already carrying. When the time is right, if you are thinking of selling, we at Transitus are happy to show you all your options. But for tonight, let the house go back to being what it has always been: the place you own, not a question you have to answer.
