Bracing for a partition action over the house

Worried about a partition action in your own family.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · October 19, 2026

One word with legal teeth just entered the family. The house was supposed to be the simple part of the inheritance. The grief is hard enough and more disagreement isn’t helping. Instead it is the thing nobody can say out loud. One of you needs the money, or lives three states away, or is quietly paying the taxes on rooms nobody uses. One of you cannot picture a stranger's car in that driveway and these are all fair. And somewhere in the stalemate, a word arrived that sounds less like family and more like court: partition. Whichever side of the deadlock you are standing on, you came for the same answer, so I will hand it to you straight.

Yes, and it is not a bluff

In Colorado, any co-owner of a property can file a court action to divide it, and that includes a single heir holding an undivided share of an inherited house (CRS 38-28-101). That filing is called a partition action. The heir who files does not need the others to agree. Not a majority of the siblings, not a family vote. One person, one filing, and the case exists.

And once it exists, it does not stay between two people. Everyone with any interest in the property has to be made a party to the case (CRS 38-28-102). One sibling filing pulls the entire family into the same courtroom, every name on the same lawsuit. If you are the one who wants out, that is your lever, and it is real. If you heard the word as a threat across a strained phone call, I am sorry, it was not a bluff. But real is not the same as inevitable. Almost everything below exists so this never has to go that far for you and your family in this tough time.

A way out that keeps Thanksgiving

Before anyone pictures a courtroom, look at what the law already lets your family do at the kitchen table. Colorado lets heirs rewrite their shares by agreement: a written agreement, signed by everyone affected, can rearrange who gets what in any way you all decide, and the personal representative has to follow it, subject to the estate's debts (CRS 15-12-912). That one rule is the legal footing under the two endings families actually want.

The first is a buyout. The sibling who wants to keep the house pays the ones who want out for their shares. The house stays in the family, and the people who need the money get it. The second is an agreed sale: everyone signs, the house sells once, on the family's timeline, and the money divides without a judge touching any of it. And when the talking itself is the broken part, families in this exact spot often bring in a mediator, a neutral person whose whole job is moving a stuck conversation onto a signed page. Needing one is not failure. Grief and money in the same room is the hardest negotiation there is. If the stuck part is nobody knowing what the house is really worth or all the paths available, we at Transitus can lay those numbers out for your family.

The house itself can be someone's share

If the estate is still open, there is a door many families miss. The estate does not have to be sold off and split as cash. Colorado allows distribution in kind, which means one heir can receive the house itself as their share while the others take other assets or money, as long as nobody objects to the proposed split (CRS 15-12-906(2)(c)). The sibling who cannot let the house go may not need to force or fight anything. The house can simply be their inheritance.

That door has a clock however, and it swings both ways. The personal representative can send everyone a written proposal for how the estate will be divided, and an heir who objects to the kind or value of what they would receive has to object in writing, received within thirty days of that mailing, or the right to object ends (CRS 15-12-906(3)). If a proposal like that is in your mail right now, the window to speak is real and it is short. If you are the one hoping a proposal holds, thirty days of silence is what lets it.

One piece of ground truth while the estate is open: the house passed to all of you at the moment of death, but it passed with strings, subject to the estate's administration and debts (CRS 15-12-101), and until it is distributed, the legal power over its title sits with the personal representative, not with any one heir (CRS 15-12-711). That is why these in-estate endings run through the representative's paperwork instead of through one sibling's say-so.

Bracing for what the courtroom version looks like

If every earlier door stays shut, here is the honest shape of the thing itself. While the estate is open, partition can even happen inside the probate case: any one heir can petition the probate court to divide estate property before the estate closes, and the court handles it the same way an ordinary partition case runs (CRS 15-12-911). Even there, a sale is not automatic. The court directs a sale only of property that cannot be divided and cannot conveniently be allotted whole to one of you. The house landing in one heir's column stays possible surprisingly deep into the fight.

In the ordinary case, the court does not simply order a sale on day one either. It appoints disinterested commissioners, people with no stake in your family's fight, who take an oath, view the property, and submit a written division for the court to confirm, and any party can file objections within the time the court sets (CRS 38-28-105). For land, dividing can mean literally drawing lines. For a single house, it almost never can.

That is where the forced sale actually comes from. When the property cannot be physically divided without real harm to someone's rights, the court may order it sold (CRS 38-28-107). One heir files, the house cannot be split, so the house gets sold. That is the entire mechanism behind the sentence. And a mortgage does not block it: the court can order the house sold free of the lien and have it paid through the case, or divide that debt among the parties (CRS 38-28-110).

The price of forcing it

Say it runs all the way to the end. The sale comes back to the court for confirmation, the court directs the deed to the buyer, and the money, along with any income from the property that was never passed out, gets divided among everyone entitled to it (CRS 38-28-108). Each heir receives their share of what is left. Hold onto those last four words.

Because before anyone's share is counted, the court sets the pay of the commissioners and of the person who ran the sale, and it can order that pay, plus costs, expenses, and attorney's fees, paid out of the sale money first (CRS 38-28-109). The process gets fed off the top, and the family splits what remains. The same house, sold by agreement instead, has none of that machinery standing between the price and your shares. That is the quiet math under every partition threat: the heir forcing the sale truly can force it, and everyone, the one who filed included, usually walks away with less than if the family had chosen the sale together.

The family is the thing worth keeping

You came here either measuring a threat or holding one, inside a grief that was supposed to pull your family together, not put them on opposite sides of a lawsuit. The house is not just an asset to any of you, and neither are the people you share it with. I hope this gets decided at a table, not in a courtroom. The house was your parent's. The ending can still be yours, all of yours.
