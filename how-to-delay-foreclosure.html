Cornered and searching how to delay foreclosure

Cornered and bargaining for more time.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · September 4, 2026

The date is on the paper now. Everything you do this week gets measured against it, and somewhere in the measuring, the ask shrank. You stopped hoping for a miracle and started hoping for a month. I know what that shrinking costs. So here is the answer, straight. Yes, you can buy time. Not unlimited time, and rarely free time, but real time, written into law, and most of the moves that buy it are still yours to make. I'm going to lay out each one and what it actually buys, so you can decide where to spend what you have left.

The relief that the sale can be held by law

The strongest lever you have is the one most people mistake for paperwork: a loss mitigation application, which is just the formal request for one of the alternatives to foreclosure your servicer offers, with every document they ask for.

Complete, and in your servicer's hands more than 37 days before the sale date, it does two things at once. It forces them to evaluate you for every option they have and answer in writing within 30 days (12 CFR 1024.41(c)(1)). And while that review is open, they cannot go through with the sale, unless they have told you in writing that nothing fits, you turn down everything offered, or you stop performing on a plan you accepted (12 CFR 1024.41(g)). In plain words: once they have your complete file on time, the sale waits until they answer you. That is not a courtesy. That is the sale being held by rule.

Colorado adds the piece that turns the rule into a date actually moving. This is where people in your spot get the servicer's written confirmation that the application was complete and on time, and put it in the public trustee's hands no later than 14 calendar days before the sale date. The trustee then has to ask your servicer about it within three business days and postpone the sale, continue is the legal word for it, until the servicer answers (CRS 38-38-103.2(3)). In plain words: that one piece of paper, delivered, is what makes the trustee hold the date. The delivery is on you, not on them. But once it happens, the auction date stops being fixed.

If the servicer confirms an option was offered, you accepted it, and you are keeping up with it, the hold becomes something better than a hold. The sale is postponed, and the foreclosure itself has to be withdrawn within 180 days as long as you keep complying (CRS 38-38-103.2(3)(d)(I)). In plain words: a workout you keep up with does not delay the foreclosure. It ends it. Time bought this way is not borrowed. One edge, told straight: if the servicer writes back disputing that any of it happened, the sale goes forward. The hold lives on their confirmation.

Two more honest pieces. Complete means their full list, not your best effort, and they have to tell you in writing what is still missing. And this protection generally runs once for each stretch of being behind, so a second application does not restart the hold (12 CFR 1024.41(i)). You get one real swing at this. Most people make it count.

Wondering if the bank is a wall or a person

Now the question underneath the calendar: is the lender someone you can actually talk to? Yes, and the law built the desk. By the 45th day of falling behind, your servicer has to give you a single point of contact, one person whose job includes telling you exactly what you must do to be evaluated and when the loan may be referred to foreclosure (CRS 38-38-103.1). That is the person the conversation happens with.

What they can hand you, short of a full answer on your application, is smaller time. A short-term forbearance, a pause or a shrinking of your payments for a stretch, or a short-term repayment plan can be offered even on an incomplete application. While you are performing on it, they cannot file a foreclosure, cannot ask the court for the order that authorizes a sale, and cannot go through with the sale (12 CFR 1024.41(c)(2)(iii)). Forbearance pauses the collecting, not the owing. Every paused payment is still owed later. But it holds the date, and for some families that is the whole month they needed.

And the biggest lever in the building belongs to them. The lender can ask the public trustee to postpone the sale at any time before it begins, and the trustee can do the same for good cause, as long as no sale moves more than twelve months past its original date (CRS 38-38-109(1)(a)). Any weeks the sale gets pushed back because of your application above do not count against that twelve months. They can even withdraw the whole foreclosure at any point before the sale. So the machine has a person in it who can say yes.

What no rule does is make them say yes. They do not have to grant anything (12 CFR 1024.41(a)). I know that is the sentence you were bracing for. It is also why the application above matters so much. It is the only part of this conversation where the deadlines belong to them and not to you.

The stop nobody can refuse you

There is one move that stops the sale without anyone's permission, and I would rather name it here than have it be the thing you find at midnight. Filing a bankruptcy petition triggers an automatic stay that halts foreclosure activity, including a sale already on the calendar (11 U.S.C. 362(a)). Colorado's trustee has to announce it on the sale date, take no action, and let the sale roll forward week to week (CRS 38-38-109(2)(a)).

Its true size, so it cannot surprise you. The lender can ask the court to lift the stay, and courts grant that for cause, including when there is no equity in the home and the home is not needed for a repayment plan through the court (11 U.S.C. 362(d)). If you had a bankruptcy case dismissed in the past year, this new stay ends 30 days after you file unless the court extends it. If two or more were dismissed, no stay takes effect at all.

It is a brake with a long price tag, and nearly everyone pulls it with an attorney beside them. I hope you never need it. You still deserve to know it is there.

Hoping the small levers buy more than they do

A few smaller moves, at their real size, so you don't overspend on them. Before the trustee can sell, the lender needs a court order under Rule 120, and you get a response deadline set 21 to 35 days after their motion is filed. If you file a written response with a real reason the rule allows, like that you are not actually behind, or that the company foreclosing is not the one that holds your loan, the court has to set a hearing. If you file nothing, the order is entered right away (C.R.C.P. 120). A response with a real basis buys a hearing. One with no basis mostly buys a hard day in court.

Catching the loan up, which Colorado calls curing, has a quiet clock in it too. If you file a written notice of intent to cure at least 15 calendar days before the sale, the lender has to produce an itemized statement of what catching up costs. If they miss their deadline for it, the trustee must push the sale back a week, and another week for each week they stay late (CRS 38-38-104(7)). And if the lender fails to get the court's order to the trustee by noon two business days before the sale, the sale is postponed (CRS 38-38-105(2)(b)).

None of these is a plan. They are the days that fall out of the machine when the other side stumbles, and I mention them so a week's reprieve means the right thing when it appears. Not a rescue. A week.

The fear every extra week is borrowed

Here is the part I most want you to carry out of this. Every week bought above, except the option you accept and actually keep up with, is borrowed, and the law is clear about where borrowing ends. Once the sale happens, there is no buying it back in Colorado (CRS 38-38-302(1)). Nothing on this page survives the sale.

So the time is not for waiting. It is for turning one of these holds into an ending, because a month is only worth what you do inside it, and you have more moves left than the date makes you feel.
