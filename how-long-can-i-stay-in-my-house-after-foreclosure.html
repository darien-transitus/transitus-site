Dreading how long you can stay after foreclosure

Counting the days you have in your home after foreclosure.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · August 6, 2026

You are not searching for a rescue tonight. You are searching for a number. Enough days to find a rental, pack the rooms, keep the kids in school until the leaving is done. And under the number is a picture you keep pushing away: a knock at the door, your things on the lawn, leaving on someone else's schedule instead of your own. I understand the emotional burden this has but I promise nobody can kick you out the morning of the foreclosure auction. So, you have more time than it may seem and I’ll show you exactly how much.

Sale day comes and you are still home

To start, the winning bidder at the auction does not own your home that day. What they walk away with is a certificate of purchase, a document recorded within five business days that only promises them a deed later (CRS 38-38-401(1)). Ownership itself, what the law calls title, exchanges hands after eight business days of the sale if no junior lienholders step forward prior (CRS 38-38-501(1)).

That is not a long runway, and I will not pretend it is. But the fear usually rounds this window down to zero, and zero is wrong. For those days, the law still has you at home. And if your sale has not even happened yet, please hear this next part the loudest. The beginning of the foreclosure process does not start the leaving and you have a chance to stay forever all the way until the end. Looking into loss mitigation or a cure path is your best chance to do it. So, if you aren’t at the feared auction day yet you still have hope and opportunity and we at Transitus are happy to show your options.

Extra days can appear, but they are not yours to make

Colorado has a redemption window that can add more time to your clock, but the redemption itself only belongs to junior lienholders. Unfortunately, you, the former owner, are not on that list (CRS 38-38-302(1)). So the days after the sale are for staying put and planning your landing, not for undoing the sale.

If one steps forward in that 8 day window, the redemption itself happens fifteen to nineteen business days after the sale, and title cannot vest until those periods run out (CRS 38-38-302(4)(a)(I)). You do not control this, and I would not count on it but it exists. If it happens, the clock stretches, and every stretched day is one more you get to live in your home.

The knock cannot come without paper first

Now the fear with the sharpest edge. The one where someone simply shows up and removes you. Colorado law does not allow that at the beginning. Full stop. Staying in the home after the sale only becomes unlawful detention after three separate things have happened: the sale itself, the new owner's title being perfected, and a demand for possession being made to you (CRS 13-40-104(1)(f)).

This demand is not a knock or a phone call. It has to be in writing, say what right they claim, describe the property, give you a date and time to move by, and carry a signature (CRS 13-40-106(1)). Until a paper like that reaches you, an eviction case cannot properly begin. The removal you keep picturing is not allowed to skip the warning. The warning is the law's first requirement and you get it too.

A place you can be but not want to be

Say the date on the demand passes and you are still home. What comes next I won't mention lightly. The new owner can file for eviction, and if it runs its full course it can end with officers removing you physically, no sooner than ten days after judgment and only in daylight (CRS 13-40-122(1)(a)-(b)). I know it can be hard to leave your home, I see that. I do. But dragging it to that point tends to cost you more, in stress and in the time it takes to get you back on your feet. Only you can weigh that. But if part of you already knows, moving on sooner is often what protects you for tomorrow.

The number you actually came for

So add it up from the auction morning forward. Title takes roughly two weeks to transfer. Only then does a written demand arrive with a move-out date. That date varies. Everything after it, the filing, the case, the waiting periods, run on their own clock. The honest range from sale day to any forced removal is about a month, often more. Not because you're fighting it. Because the next step for you may take longer than you hope and all the time available is important to know. I tell you this so the ground feels solid under you. Not so you plan your life around the last possible day.

The 90 days you may have read about

One correction before you go, because the internet hands this number to everyone in your spot. There is a federal rule giving 90 days of notice after a foreclosure, and it is real, but it protects renters not you the former owner. The law is explicit that the protection does not apply when the tenant is the person who took out the mortgage, or that person's spouse, child, or parent (Protecting Tenants at Foreclosure Act, Pub. L. 111-22, sec. 702). I would rather you hear that here than have you plan three months around a rule that was never yours to begin with. I know this can be a let down but truth is more important so you plan this correctly.

The leaving can still be yours

You came here for a number because the number is the last thing you can still control. Now you have it, along with all the warnings the law makes. The days are real. Nothing has to happen in the dark. But it still may not feel fair and that is a completely valid place to end. However the leaving goes, I hope it goes gently, and on a day you chose.
