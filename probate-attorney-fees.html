Dreading what probate attorney fees add up to

Afraid probate will eat what they left you.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · October 31, 2026

There is a notepad somewhere near you with law firm names on it. You have been making the calls quietly, asking each office what they charge, in the same stretch of days you are still answering condolence texts. It feels mercenary and you keep doing it anyway, because the stories reached you first. The estate that went into probate and came out smaller. The lawyer who took a piece of everything. The inheritance spent on the receiving of it. You are not here for comfort. You want the actual bill, named plainly, so you can stop budgeting against the worst story. That is exactly what I am going to hand you.

The percentage you keep hearing about is not the law here

Take the worst version off the table first. Colorado does not set probate attorney fees as a percentage of the estate. There is no statutory fee schedule at all. What the law entitles a personal representative and their lawyer to is reasonable compensation for the services actually rendered (CRS 15-10-602(1)). Reasonable, for the work done. Not a slice of everything your parent owned.

So when an office quotes you, it will be an hourly rate or a flat fee, and neither is automatically the wrong shape. Every billing method is judged against the same measure of reasonableness, and when a court weighs a fee, it has to consider things like the time and labor the work required, what is customarily charged in the community for similar work, and the nature and size of the estate (CRS 15-10-603(3)). I know you came for a sticker price, and a standard does not spend like a number. But hold what the standard means. The bill has to answer to the work, not to the size of what they left you.

The court's share turns out to be the small part

Now the lines you can actually write down. As of 2026, the filing fee to open a regular decedent's estate in Colorado is $229. A small estate files for $113, depositing a will with the court costs $18, and the Judicial Branch lists no fee at all for closing an estate (CRS 13-32-102). These numbers get adjusted from time to time, but the scale is the point. The court itself charges a few hundred dollars, not a percentage of anything.

And two lines you can strike off the ledger entirely. Colorado has no estate tax for current deaths and no inheritance tax. The federal estate tax is real, but a return is only required when the estate is worth more than the filing threshold for the year of death, $15,000,000 for 2026. For nearly every family doing this math, the taxes you have been bracing for are not in this bill at all.

The basis comes to you in writing, up front

Here is a protection built for exactly the shopping you have been doing. In Colorado, the basis for any fees charged to the estate has to be disclosed at the start: the application or petition that opens the estate must state how compensation will be charged, such as specific hourly rates or a published fee schedule, or say plainly that the basis has not been determined yet. If that basis materially changes later, the duty to disclose continues (CRS 15-10-602(9)).

So those quiet phone calls are not you being cold. Comparing what two offices charge is part of how a reasonable number gets found, and families in your position make the same calls every week. And the law stays in the room after the hiring too. Fees can generally be paid from the estate as the work happens, without a court order, but the court always keeps its authority to review them, and anyone who received excessive compensation must be ordered to refund it (CRS 15-10-602(5)). The bill is never beyond question, even after it is paid.

The checks come from the estate, not from you

Now the fear about whose pocket this empties. Probate costs, the attorney's fees and the personal representative's compensation included, are paid by the estate itself. And they are not paid last, out of whatever happens to survive. In the order Colorado sets for paying an estate's obligations, the costs of administration stand near the front of the line, ahead of funeral expenses, the medical bills of the final illness, and every other debt (CRS 15-12-805(1)(b)).

I know the practical version of this worry too. The accounts are frozen, nothing has been sold, and someone still has to hand the court its filing fee this month. If you front a cost like that, the estate is where the money comes back from. You are advancing the estate's own bill, not donating to it. One more charge that usually never appears: a bond. In an ordinary informal probate, no bond is required of the personal representative except in narrow cases, such as a will that expressly demands one (CRS 15-12-603).

The honest reason nobody quotes one total

Here is what the standard from earlier actually turns on: time. Estates take different amounts of it, and even a smooth, unsupervised Colorado estate cannot be closed earlier than six months after the personal representative's appointment, or one year after the death, whichever comes first (CRS 15-12-1003(1)). An hourly bill grows across those months. A flat fee prices them in ahead of time. Either way, the calendar is inside the number.

The rest of the bill is made of smaller, predictable pieces. Publishing the notice to creditors in a county newspaper, once a week for three straight weeks, is a standard expense of nearly every estate. An appraisal is an authorized one, for any asset whose value is genuinely in doubt (CRS 15-12-707), and in most families that asset is the house. The route matters too: informal probate, where the registrar's appointment fully establishes the personal representative's authority without any court hearing, is the lower-cost road ordinary estates travel (CRS 15-12-307(2)). Fewer hearings means fewer billed hours. The estates in the horror stories are usually the fought-over ones, where a family dispute, not the process, is what the money went to.

The version where the bill nearly disappears

One more door to check before you hire anyone, because the cheapest probate is the one that never opens. Colorado's small-estate affidavit collects a modest estate without probate at all, which means without the process the fees attach to. It has firm edges. It covers personal property only, things like bank funds and belongings, never real estate.

The whole estate, less liens, has to fit under a cap tied to the year of death: $88,000 for a death in 2026, $86,000 for 2025. And it is available only once ten days have passed and no personal representative has been appointed or applied for (CRS 15-12-1201(1)). If the estate is a house, this door is not yours. If it is a bank account and a car, you may not need to price probate at all. The house has doors of its own. If it was already sitting in a living trust, or if a surviving co-owner was on the deed, it passes outside probate entirely, and there is no probate bill to price.

Careful with their money, even now

You have been comparing prices in one of the hardest weeks there is, and feeling strange about it the whole time. Set that part down. It is you protecting what they meant you to have, all the way to the end. If a house is part of what the estate holds, we at Transitus are happy to show you your options whenever you are ready.
