Unsure how to sell a house in trust after death

Named in the trust, wondering how to sell the house.

Written by Transitus · Denver, Colorado · October 18, 2026

Years ago, a parent sat down with a lawyer and planned for this exact week. The living trust was the plan: no probate, no court, nothing for the family to untangle. Now the week is here. A week that’s already hard enough for you. A loss of someone deeply important, a house full of their life, a document with your name somewhere inside it, and no help to guide you through this process. So let me hand you the whole answer before any of the details. In Colorado, the house is sold by the trust's trustee, that trustee is now you, and no judge has to approve any of it. The plan your parents made still works. The rest is how you carry it out.

The fear that irrevocable means frozen

Somewhere in your reading you probably met the sentence that stops most people: the living trust became irrevocable when they died. The word sounds like a vault closing, but here is what actually happened. While your parent was alive, the trust was theirs to change, because Colorado lets the person who created a trust revoke or amend it unless its terms say otherwise (CRS 15-5-602(1)). The day they died, the one person with the power to change it was gone. I know that part doesn’t feel like it matters today and it doesn’t have to. But that is all irrevocable means. Their wishes are now fixed. The house is not.

The proof sits in the same body of law. A Colorado trustee holds the specific power to sell trust property, at a public or private sale (CRS 15-5-816(1)(b)). And the trustee can use that power without asking a court first: over the trust's property, a trustee has the powers an outright owner would have, unless the trust's own terms cut them back (CRS 15-5-815(1)).

Making sure the pen is really yours

Before anything gets signed, one quiet question deserves a plain answer: who is the trustee now? Your parents most likely held that job themselves while they were alive. Their death left the seat empty, and Colorado fills it in a set order: first the successor trustee the trust names, then a person all the qualified beneficiaries unanimously agree on, then a person the court appoints (CRS 15-5-704). For nearly every family in your position, it never gets past the first one. The document names a successor, and the successor is you. There is no swearing-in, no courthouse visit to collect a badge.

The stranger who will ask you to prove it

Colorado built a document for exactly the moment you need to prove your authority, called a certification of trust. It is short. It says the trust exists, who created it, who the current trustee is, and what powers the trustee has for this transaction. It does not have to reveal the part your family may want kept private, who inherits what (CRS 15-5-1013).

And the law leans on your side of the table. Anyone who relies on your certification in good faith is protected, and anyone who demands the entire trust instrument without good faith is on the hook for costs, attorney fees, and damages. So the closing table is not an interrogation waiting to happen. The deed sits in the trust's name, you sign as its trustee, and one short paper is what backs you up.

A sale that turns out to be almost ordinary

With the authority settled, the rest is close to an ordinary sale: list the house, take offers, negotiate, close. One piece of the machinery that scares people more than it should: the mortgage. If a loan is still on the house, it did not come due when your parent died. Federal law stops a lender from calling the loan just because a home moved into a living trust, or because a relative received it at the borrower's death (12 U.S.C. 1701j-3(d)). The sale you are planning is the thing that finally pays it: the loan gets paid off at closing, out of the buyer's money, like any ordinary sale. The monthly payments however keep coming due in the meantime, which is one honest reason families in your seat tend to keep the sale moving rather than letting the house sit. Doing it clean while the family is watching

As the trustee, you have a burden to carry and I see it. The beneficiaries are probably your own siblings and you want to do this right for them and the law. Colorado already drew the lines to keep you safe. As trustee you act in good faith, follow the trust's terms, treat the beneficiaries impartially, and handle the property the way a prudent person would (CRS 15-5-801, 15-5-803, 15-5-804). In plain terms: the sale money is the trust's, not yours, a real price beats a quick favor, and nobody gets a side deal.

Two letters keep it in the open. Within sixty days of accepting the job, the qualified beneficiaries get notice that you took it, with your name, address, and phone number. And within sixty days after you learn the trust became irrevocable by death, they get notice of the trust's existence, who created it, their right to request the portions of the trust that affect them, and their right to a trustee's report (CRS 15-5-813(2)(b)-(c)). I know two more letters sound like homework on top of grief. But those letters protect you as much as anyone.

When the sale is done, the money moves the same quiet way the house did: the trustee can distribute the trust property under its terms without any court proceeding (CRS 15-5-604(2)). And if a corner of you fears someone surfacing years from now to contest it all, Colorado puts a hard edge there too. A challenge to the trust has to be filed within three years of the death, or within 120 days after you send someone a copy of the trust with the proper notice, whichever comes first, and that deadline cannot be waived (CRS 15-5-604(1)). The window for a fight is real. It also closes so you can move on with your life during this hard time.

The tax ambush that mostly is not coming

You may be bracing for the government's share of a house your parents bought decades ago. For most families in exactly your position, the bill is far smaller than the fear. A home held in a revocable living trust gets what tax law calls a stepped-up basis: for tax purposes, its cost resets to the home's value on the day the owner died (26 U.S.C. 1014(a)). The decades of growth your parents lived through are not your taxable gain. If the house sells near that date-of-death value, there may be little or nothing left to tax. And for deaths after 2004, Colorado has no state estate tax waiting behind the federal rules.

One edge I will not hide, so it cannot ambush you later. That reset belongs to trusts that were revocable, the ordinary living trust. A trust your parent deliberately made irrevocable while they were still alive plays by harder rules: the IRS has ruled that assets in an irrevocable trust sitting outside the taxable estate do not get the step-up (Rev. Rul. 2023-2). If your papers say the trust was irrevocable from the start, that is the one corner of this that families almost always hand to a tax professional rather than carry alone.

Carrying out the plan they made for you

Your parents planned this so the hardest season of your life would have one easy part, and you have been careful enough to learn the job before signing a thing. That is you honoring them, not just handling an asset. I hope the sale goes gently, and I hope what they really left you is more than a house.
